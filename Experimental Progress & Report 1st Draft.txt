our insurance-claim screening experiment. We ran three models (Gemma 3 4B-it, 12B-it, and 27B-it) 
across seven policy formatting conditions (placebo plus six structured formats: prose, bullet points,

analysis includes multinomial logistic regression models, likelihood ratio tests for interaction

across formats, and preliminary fairness checks (e.g., approval rate by gender and other covariates). 
While interpretation of results is still in progress, the analytical framework is now fully implemented 
and ready for refinement.

# Introduction

Large language models are increasingly deployed in decision-support systems across domains such as 
healthcare, finance, and insurance. While these systems are often presented as neutral and objective, 
real-world reporting has raised concerns about automated decision pipelines influencing high-stakes 
outcomes. For example, recent investigations have highlighted the use of algorithmic systems in health 
insurance claim screening and alleged wrongful denials of care (CBS News, 2023; The Guardian, 2025). 
These cases illustrate how automated systems can materially affect patient access to medical treatment 
and financial stability.

Beyond overt bias, an additional concern is decision instability: whether small variations in rule 
presentation or language structure influence model outputs. Prior research on large language models 
has demonstrated sensitivity to prompt phrasing and framing, suggesting that outputs may vary even when 
the underlying task remains logically identical. If formatting alone alters decision patterns, this 
raises questions about the reliability of LLMs in structured decision-making contexts.

In this study, we investigate whether the formatting of insurance decision rules affects the behavior 
of LLMs when screening medical claims. Rather than modifying the substantive content of policies, we 
hold policy meaning constant while varying presentation style. We analyze whether formatting influences 
overall approval rates, the relationship between claimant features and decisions, and the stability of 
outcomes across conditions.


# Experimental Design

We constructed a synthetic dataset of 100 insurance claims inspired by real-world medical claim forms 
(e.g., CMS-1500 structure). Each claim includes structured covariates such as:
 - Name
 -	Age
 -	Gender
 -	Relationship to insured
 -	Diagnosis
 -	Service type
 -	Severity level
 -	Preexisting condition status
 -	In-network status
 -	Prior authorization requirement and completion
 -	Documentation completeness
 -	Claim amount

These variables were chosen to reflect factors commonly considered in insurance review.

## Policy Conditions
We designed seven policy conditions:
	0.	Placebo (no policy instructions provided)
	1.	Prose format
	2.	Bullet-point format
	3.	Decision tree format
	4.	Bias-emphasis format (explicit non-discrimination emphasis)
	5.	Minimal summary format
	6.	Verbose, legally detailed format

Each condition contains substantively identical approval criteria (medical necessity, coverage, 
documentation requirements, etc.), differing only in presentation structure.

## Prompting Procedure
For each experiment, the LLM receives:
 -	A structured claim description
 -	A policy text corresponding to the condition (or blank for placebo)

The model is instructed to return a single categorical decision:
 -	APPROVE
 -	DENY
 -	NEED_MORE_INFO

We restrict outputs to a single label to minimize variation due to explanatory text and simplify 
statistical analysis.

## Models and Trials
We evaluate three models:
 -	Gemma 3 4B-it
 -	Gemma 3 12B-it
 -	Gemma 3 27B-it

Each model evaluates 100 claims under all 7 policy conditions, resulting in:

  3 models × 7 conditions × 100 claims = 2,100 total model decisions.

All experiments were run via the Gemini API using consistent prompting structure.

## Analysis Plan
We treat the model’s decision as a categorical response variable and conduct:
 -	Multinomial logistic regression to estimate relationships between claimant features and decisions.
 -	Likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without policy-format interaction terms.
 -	Instability analysis measuring how often decisions change across policy formats.
 -	Fairness metrics examining whether demographic variables (e.g., gender) are associated with 
differential approval rates, controlling for clinical variables.

Our primary question is whether policy formatting significantly moderates the relationship between 
claimant attributes and model decisions.
